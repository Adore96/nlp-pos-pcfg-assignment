    URL: https://www.dailymirror.lk/international/Chinas-youth-enemployment-hits-record-high-as-AI-deepens-job-crunch/107-350984...
    Nepal Aaja - China’s youth unemployment rate climbed to 18.9 per cent in August, highlighting the growing difficulties facing a record number of university graduates entering an increasingly competitive labour market. Weak domestic demand, slowing economic momentum and concerns over artificial intelligence (AI) replacing or reshaping entry-level roles are adding to the employment pressures.

Data released by the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) last week showed that the unemployment rate among 16–24-year-olds, excluding students, rose from 17.9 per cent in July to 18.9 per cent in August. The figure matched the rate recorded in August last year and remains among the highest since China introduced its revised youth unemployment methodology in 2023.

Under the earlier methodology, youth unemployment had reached 21.3 per cent in June 2023. The revised calculation excludes students, making direct comparisons with earlier figures difficult.

Record Graduate Influx Meets a Weak Job Market

An estimated 12.7 million university students graduated this summer, adding substantial pressure to a labour market already affected by subdued economic activity. The annual rise in youth unemployment is partly seasonal, as new graduates enter the workforce and take time to secure employment.

However, the scale of the graduate influx raises broader questions about the ability of China’s  economy to generate sufficient skilled jobs, particularly in sectors traditionally viewed as entry points for educated young workers.

The technology sector, in particular, is undergoing changes as companies increasingly adopt AI-powered tools. While AI can improve productivity and create new occupations, its impact on junior positions has become a source of anxiety among recent graduates competing for limited opportunities.

Regional Exposure to AI

The potential employment impact of AI extends beyond China. A report published by the International Labour Organisation (ILO) last month found that approximately 7.5 per cent of jobs held by workers aged 15–29 in East Asia are in occupations highly exposed to AI.

The agency estimated that if 10 per cent of those positions disappeared, around 1.5 million young people across the region could face unemployment, move into other occupations or leave the workforce altogether.

The figures highlight the importance of distinguishing between exposure to AI and actual job displacement. Occupations affected by AI may undergo changes in their tasks and skill requirements rather than disappear entirely. Nevertheless, workers entering the labour market without substantial experience could face particular difficulties if employers reduce junior recruitment or raise their technical expectations.

Broader Labour Market Pressures

The deterioration in youth employment coincided with a modest increase in unemployment among older young workers. The jobless rate for those aged 25–29, excluding students, rose to 7.5 per cent in August from 7.2 per cent in July.

China’s overall urban unemployment rate also edged up to 5.3 per cent in August, compared with 5.2 per cent in July.

Taken together, the figures point to continued pressure in China’s labour market, although the youth unemployment rate is particularly sensitive to seasonal graduate entry and changes in job-search patterns.

The immediate challenge is to absorb the latest cohort of graduates while sustaining employment opportunities in sectors capable of supporting skilled workers. Over the longer term, the interaction between weak domestic demand, industrial restructuring and AI adoption could shape the employment prospects of a generation entering the workforce amid significant economic and technological change.

      URL: https://www.dailymirror.lk/news-features/Is-Nature-Finally-Striking-Back-at-Us/131-350945...

    Of late, one cannot help feeling that natural disasters are occurring around us with increasing frequency.

Almost every time we switch on the television or look at the news and social media, there seems to be another natural disaster somewhere in the world.

Sri Lanka has had its own share, with the most recent being the devastating effects of Ditwah and subsequent extreme weather. Nepal has experienced destructive floods and landslides, while the Philippines, Myanmar, Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Venezuela and Colombia have all faced different natural calamities in recent times.

Floods, cyclones, landslides, droughts, wildfires and extreme heat seem to be occurring with regularity. Earthquakes have added to the destruction in some countries, although they belong to a very different geological process.

Against this background, a serious question inevitably arises: is nature striking back at us?

It is a tempting thought, particularly when one remembers Conservation International’s Nature is Speaking campaign, where Julia Roberts, as the voice of Mother Nature, reminds us rather chillingly that nature has been here for billions of years, far longer than human beings, and will continue long after us (2014 Conservation International). The central message is brutally simple: nature does not need us, but we need nature.

There is something chilling about this because we humans have spent much of our relatively short existence behaving as though the opposite were true. Somewhere along the way we seem to have convinced ourselves that we are all-powerful and in full control.

Have We Begun to Think We Are God?

Perhaps we can even be excused for this arrogance. After all, have we not sent men to the moon and brought them safely back? We have split the atom, changed DNA, cloned mammals and, more recently, grown human embryo-like models in laboratories using stem cells, without the normal process of fertilisation involving sperm and an egg. We can transplant hearts, communicate instantly across the world, look billions of years back into the universe. And most recently we have now built machines capable of forms of reasoning that, until very recently, we thought belonged only to us.   These are astonishing achievements by any measure, and perhaps it is understandable that somewhere along this extraordinary journey of human advancement, we began to believe that there was very little beyond our control. In fact, we may perhaps be excused for occasionally thinking that we are God.

But despite all this extraordinary knowledge and technological capability, our influence over the great forces of nature remains surprisingly limited.   We have become increasingly sophisticated in predicting natural events and protecting ourselves from some of their consequences, but the forces that create cyclones, earthquakes, monsoons, floods and rising seas remain essentially beyond our control. We may be able to predict some of these events increasingly well, protect ourselves against some of their effects and sometimes reduce the damage they cause, but we certainly cannot control nature itself (2016, Yuval Noah Harari).

Perhaps our scientific achievements have therefore given us a rather dangerous illusion. We have become very good at manipulating parts of nature and, somewhere along the way, begun confusing that ability with mastery over nature itself.

But Is Nature Really Striking Back?

Scientifically, of course, the answer has to be no. Nature does not have feelings, become angry with us or decide that humans have misbehaved and therefore send a cyclone, flood or landslide as punishment.

Nor should we fall into the increasingly common habit, encouraged by some climate change doomsday prophets, of attributing every natural disaster to climate change. Take earthquakes, for example, which are primarily geological events arising from movements within the Earth’s crust. Floods, cyclones, droughts, landslides and wildfires also occurred for millions of years before humans started burning fossil fuels on an industrial scale. The Earth has always been dynamic and, at times, extremely violent as it goes through its cycles of evolution.   It would therefore be overly simplistic, and scientifically wrong, to say that all the disasters we are seeing today have been caused by humans.

But that certainly does not exonerate us by any means, because we have definitely altered the conditions in which many natural events now occur. Human-induced climate change has warmed the atmosphere, oceans and land, and the evidence is now very strong that this warming is influencing many weather and climate extremes.

Rising sea levels worsen coastal flooding and storm surges, while prolonged heat and drought can leave landscapes much more vulnerable to wildfires. (2021, IPCC AR6 Working Group I- “Weather and Climate Extreme Events in a Changing Climate”)   However, long before climate change became the great environmental issue of our time, we were already busy dismantling many of nature’s own defences.

We Have Weakened Nature’s Defences

For generations we have cut forests from mountain slopes, filled wetlands, destroyed mangroves, interfered with rivers, fragmented ecosystems and covered enormous areas of land with buildings, roads and concrete. When the rains come and mountainsides collapse, we then call it a natural disaster. Forests that once slowed runoff and helped hold soil together have disappeared from many areas, and we seem surprised when torrential rain sends water, mud and debris rushing down the mountains. The recent landslides after heavy rains in many of our upcountry areas are classic examples.

Similarly, we have removed mangroves and other coastal vegetation without sufficiently considering the protection these natural barriers can provide. The 2004 tsunami also demonstrated the importance of retaining natural coastal buffers.   So while the rain causes the flooding, our own actions may have helped turn that flood into a disaster. This distinction is sometimes lost in the climate change debate because a natural hazard and a human disaster are not quite the same thing. Nature may cause the hazard, but the scale of the eventual catastrophe is determined by how well prepared we are and how vulnerable the people in its path are.   Perhaps one of our more fundamental errors has been to treat forests, rivers, oceans, wetlands, wildlife, soils and climate as though they were separate entities, when in reality they form interconnected ecosystems in which disturbing one component can have consequences elsewhere.   A forest holds soil, influences water flows, stores carbon and provides habitat; wetlands hold and slowly release enormous quantities of water; mangroves reduce coastal erosion and provide breeding grounds for marine life; and oceans absorb vast quantities of heat and carbon. If we remove or seriously alter enough of these components, eventually the wider system begins to behave differently.

Yet we still talk rather confidently about “managing wildlife”, “managing forests” and “managing natural resources”, almost as though nature were some badly run corporation waiting for humans to take charge. Sometimes I wonder whether it is we who require more management.

Does the Planet Really Need Saving?

We constantly hear appeals that we must “save the planet”, an expression I have always found rather strange, because the planet will almost certainly survive us.   Earth has endured massive volcanic eruptions, asteroid impacts, ice ages, enormous climatic changes and several mass extinction events in which species disappeared, others evolved, ecosystems collapsed and different ones eventually developed.   If human beings disappeared tomorrow, forests would gradually reclaim abandoned cities, rivers would find their own paths, and wildlife would return to places from which we drove it. Evolution would continue doing what it has done for billions of years and the Earth would carry on quite well without us.   So perhaps we have the problem the wrong way round. We are not really trying to save the planet but trying to maintain the relatively narrow range of environmental conditions under which human civilisation, settlements and economies have developed, and can continue to function.   Seen in this context, the Mother Nature message becomes much harder to dismiss because, however much we may dislike admitting it, nature does not need us nearly as much as we need nature.   There is also a disturbing unfairness in all this because many of those who contributed least to climate change are among those most exposed to its consequences.   A poor family beside a river in Bangladesh, a mountain community in Nepal and a farmer in a remote village in Sri Lanka, have contributed very little to the accumulation of greenhouse gases generated during two centuries of industrialisation.    Yet they can lose their homes and livelihoods in a few hours. Richer countries generally have stronger infrastructure, better forecasting and warning systems, insurance, emergency services and greater financial resources to rebuild, while poorer countries often do not have these in sufficient measure.

Are We Asking the Wrong Question

Coming back to where we started, I don’t really believe nature is striking back at us because nature is not a person and is inanimate. What we are experiencing is something less dramatic, but perhaps more concerning. We are beginning to live with the consequences of interfering with immensely complex natural systems, that we still only partly understand.

(All images are from the internet with due acknowledgment to original owners)

Perhaps our greatest mistake has therefore been to imagine that we are somehow masters of this planet, standing above nature and deciding how much of it we can use, modify or destroy. In reality we are very recent arrivals, living within a natural system that operated for billions of years before us, and will probably continue for billions of years after us.

So perhaps nature is not striking back at us after all. We are now beginning to experience some of those consequences and, perhaps, what we are seeing is simply nature presenting us with the bill.

    The Sri Lankan economy is not an island. We like to think that all will be fine as long as we stabilise our economy and ensure fiscal discipline. However, in reality, economic crises in Sri Lanka have more often been triggered by external shocks rather than internal problems. And now we are staring at economic and political developments that signal a dangerous world.

There are different external shocks that have been in the making, and it is indeed difficult to predict as to which particular source or combination of factors can rock the global order. The war in Iran has again intensified, global oil prices are hovering around US$ 100 per barrel, shipping around the Middle East is in trouble and the region itself could spiral into escalating wars.

The racist political right is on the rise in Europe, including in France and Germany that hold Europe together. Global trade is precarious with Trump’s arbitrary tariffs over the last year and a half. Indeed, with the trade system in disarray, the possibility of an export-led economic strategy has also become questionable, because even if there is increase in comparative productivity, tariffs will nullify the advances.

In such crisis prone times, this column focuses on the dangers ahead with global financial markets. With inflation rising due to commodity price increases including oil, the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan are increasing their interest rates. Furthermore, bond yields in the US, UK, France, and Japan are the highest in decades, signalling a shift towards higher cost of capital. This would in effect, lead to even higher interest costs for developing countries like ours.

There is an even more insidious danger with the possibility of a financial meltdown with the bursting of the AI bubble. It is the crisis tendencies generated by the global financial system, of which we know little about and hard to analyse, that we need to be particularly concerned.

Repeated crises

The last five decades of neoliberal policies – what are commonly known in Sri Lanka as open economy reforms – have led to both increased financialisation of the economies and repeated financial crises. In other words, the rapid rise of financial institutions including the encroachment of finance into every sector has produced mounting crises caused by increased sovereign debt and capital flight. Notable are the Latin American and African debt crisis of the 1980s, the East Asian crisis of 1997, the North Atlantic financial crisis of 2008, the Greek sovereign debt crisis of the 2010s, and the Global South debt crisis of the early 2020s; the latter as we know crippled our country. These are symptomatic of a global financial system that creates tremendous wealth for global financiers but leads to dispossession for the rest of the population.

On a personal note, I experienced first-hand both the exuberance and the collapse of such financial crises in the 1990s and 2000s, when I worked as a telecommunications engineer in the heart of financial centres in Tokyo and New York. Even as I started working as a fresh graduate in 1994, the bond markets, particularly in the US, crashed. It took some time for the American financial sector to find its confidence, but capital found new pastures in East Asia. Three years later from the proximity of Tokyo, I observed the East Asian crisis in 1997. The crisis was propelled by the flight of capital that became possible with liberalisation of the capital account. However, global finance capital was not blamed, rather, the so-called East Asian miracles were labelled overnight by West as “crony capitalists”.

As an engineer building fiber optic networks that came of age with the expansion of the internet in the late 1990s, I heard fantastic stories of how with communication moving at the speed of light, the internet was going to completely change the global economy. Production of goods were apparently secondary, and it was information technology that would determine everything. Start-ups and massive investments, and so many computer scientists and engineers, were all lured by this so-called historic change that came to be known as the dot-com boom. But alas, in 2000 the bubble burst with so many companies going under.

Twenty-five years later, I sense similar sentiments and developments with the AI boom. The financial sector has been leveraged to the hilt for speculative investments in firms and data centres that for the most part are still loss making and can hardly show any real revenues. With every effort to hype up AI running out of steam, the time is ripe for a crash. But very few people seem to remember the past or learn from it. Indeed, finance moves quickly these days, but it also seems to make us forget quickly.

Different this time

The world economy clearly is in trouble, and I would argue it is the heavily financialised character of our contemporary global order that makes it most vulnerable to severe crises. Karl Marx, close to a century and a half ago, called financial assets that now rule our world fictitious capital. Indeed, we may have to face a great crash and tremendous economic destruction when such large amassed speculative investments meet the reality of profits and losses that are the underlying logic of capitalism.

One may believe that this may just be another crisis for global capital that in a year or two will recover like all the other crises during the neoliberal era. However, I argue that this time it is different because neoliberalism itself is in crisis. Ideologically and materially, free markets are quickly crumbling as a polarised world not just in terms of political outlooks but also in terms of trade wars, if not physical wars, which are making any coordinated response by global capital to save itself unworkable.

In other words, I claim that the consequences of another bond market crash or the AI bubble bursting will be far worse than the previous financial crises. The confluence of crisis tendencies may create a downward cycle of unravelling that even the Central Banks and Finance Ministries of the most powerful countries will be unable to address; all the more so, as these states are at loggerheads with each other. This global political situation is more akin to the situation in the 1930s, when the resolution to the crisis came only after a prolonged depression, the rise of fascism and a devastating world war.

Amidst this tumultuous world, Sri Lanka needs to rapidly change its economic policies. We need to put up barriers to the devastating flows of capital rather than go along with the IMF and its recommendations for further financial opening. We need to focus on building our production economy rather than be deluded that the financial sector, whether it be the capital markets, finance companies or banks, will guide and strengthen our economy.

Most of all, we need to shore up our resources and secure the essential needs for our people starting with food security. In a dangerously shifting world, when amnesia of past crises is the norm, are we ready to change our thinking and the economic trajectory?

    The quip that “once the mud is washed off, the man working in a paddy field is fit to be king” which famously evolved into the colloquial paraphrase, did not originate from a Lankan familiar with local parlance. It came instead from Robert Knox, an Englishman, who spent nineteen years as a prisoner in the then Kandyan kingdom before escaping and writing his celebrated work “An Historical Relation of the island Ceylon.” It noted that “taking a ploughman from Udunuwara or Yatinuwara and washing the dirt off would make him fit for kingship.”

Although he limited the leadership potential of the farmer to two specific regions, Knox’s remark has endured for centuries. In fact, it re-echoed recently in Parliament when the mounting crisis of  paddy cultivators ignited a heated debate illustrating the continued usage of Knox’s quote.

Published in London in 1681, “An Historical Relation of the island Ceylon,” was the oldest major English language book which broadened the comprehension of the  Lankan way of life. It provided European readers with an unprecedented insight into Sri Lankan society, culture and the economy during the seventeenth century long before the British established control over the island.

What makes Knox’s episode particularly significant is that his captivity   which led him to write the book was linked with a major turning point in Lankan history. In fact, if not for the unforeseen turn of events  that occurred which made the reigning King, Rajasinghe 11 (1629-1687CE) ill-tempered, “increasingly moody, suspicious and tyrannical and caused him to develop a passion to imprison Europeans,” Knox would not have become a victim of royal wrath, and circumstances would not have led him to write the celebrated book.

Literally, it was one of the earliest works written based on firsthand experience. It introduced Sinhala words such as betel, veddah, rattan, wandura and rilawa to the Oxford English Dictionary, was translated into German in 1689, Dutch in 1692 and French in 1693 and having inspired Daniel Defoe to write his famous novel “Robinson Cruso,” the book merits deeper discourse and renewed attention during the current Literary Month.

The political upheaval behind Knox’s captivity

As to his fate, Knox, in his autobiography recalled: “The Ann being hit by a storm, was forced ashore…..that fatal voyage in which I lost my father and myself and the prime of my time……”  Knox’s father and the crew of sixteen sailors were awaiting repairs in the Kottiyar Bay in the east coast not knowing that they had arrived during a period of political turmoil. An ambitious strategy employed by King Rajasinghe to expel the Portuguese from areas under their rule had misfired. The failure of his political gamble resulted in a major transition in  Lankan political history. The move in fact, led  Portugese rule in Sri Lanka’s coastal area to be replaced by the Dutch.

The unexpected development in which the Dutch kept the Portuguese territory they won instead of handing them over to the King as promised, transformed the King to a “disgruntled terror” as Knox described. The new development led people to coin the phrase “giving ginger and receiving chillies in return.”

Knox in his book described the situation vividly:  “Great vexation they gave the King by their irruptions into his Dominions. Great mischief they did him, the often times great loss on their sides. Great battles have been lost and won between them with great destruction of men on both parts. But being greatly distressed, at last he (Rajasinghe) sent and called in the Hollanders to his aid.”

Of the transition of power Knox wrote: The present King being wearied and  overmatched with the Portugese, sent for them (The Dutch) into his aid long ago from Batavia. And they did him good service,  but they feathered their own nests by the means and are now possessed of all sea coasts and the considerable Territories there unto adjoining.“

Historian K. M. de Silva in “A History of Sri Lanka” explained as to how the situation developed to a point where Rajasinghe’s sole objective became the expulsion of the Portuguese. To achieve this goal,  he was willing to pay a heavy price. K. M. de Silva wrote: The Portuguese, confined to the West Coast since they landed in 1505, had gradually strengthened their position. The conquest of the Jaffnapatan gave them to control over the lucrative pearl fisheries and the island’s elephant trade. Jaffna at the time, was the main market for elephants captured in the island.

With the interest to expand, the Portugese had been over the years attempting to make intrusions to the Kandyan Kingdom. Their attempt to install Dona Catherina as the reigning Monarch was frustrated by Konnappu Bandara who  married  her and secured his claim to the Kandyan Kingdom. Having installed himself as Vimaladharmasuriya I, (1591-1604CE), he strengthened the Kandyan Kingdom defeating Portugese hopes of conquering Kandy.

Senarath (1604-1635CE), his successor,  continued Vimaladharmasuriya’s policy of being anti-Portugese. Nevertheless, in order to prevent Portugese intrusions to the Kandyan Kingdom, Senarath signed a treaty with the Portugese in 1617 and acknowledged their authority over the maritime provinces. According to K.M. de Silva, Senarath even agreed to pay tribute to the Portugese with a promise to deny entry to their enemies.

The Dutch Alliance and its Consequences

According to “A History of Sri Lanka,” hopes of the Portuguese to subjugate Kandy renewed with the arrival of the Dutch in the Asian waters. Although this followed with regular forays, the Kandyan Kingdom however, continued to retain its independence.

It was with the ascension of Rajasinghe II that signs of change in the Kandyan policy towards the Portugese appeared. Having resorted to a more aggressive policy, he conducted intrusions deep into Portugese held areas with the aim of driving them out of the island.

In the first Treaty signed in 1633 with the Portuguese however, Rajasinghe recognised Portugese control over the eastern ports. His mood appeared to have changed as the King, adopting a more aggressive policy, sought foreign assistance to drive out the Portugese. This led him to commence negotiations with the Dutch which culminated in the Treaty of 1638.

The Dutch rapidly captured Trincomalee and Batticaloa in 1639 from the Portuguese and handed them over to King Rajasinghe. However, when the Dutch won over Galle and Negombo (in 1640) they refused to transfer control claiming that the Kandyan ruler had not paid the agreed military expenses for the previous expedition. As historian K. M. de Silva notes,   the eastern ports were not major cinnamon producing areas,  whereas Galle and Negombo commanded access to some of the richest cinnamon lands on the island.

In 1656, the Portugese Fort in Colombo surrendered,  but it took two more years to eliminate them completely,  ending over 150 years of Portugese presence in the island. The last to capitulate was the Jaffna Fort.

The English connection and Knox’s captivity

Well aware of the growing tension between the Dutch and the Kandyans, the English East India Company, doing trade in the Asian waters, sought opportunities to establish a trading presence in Sri Lanka. Their negotiations with Rajasinghe focused particularly on obtaining access to Trincomalee. Knox’s vessel “The Ann” which arrived on the eastern coast in 1659, was detained by the Kandyans followed by another in 1680, the English thus becoming victims of the increasingly complex political rivalry among the Dutch, the English and the Kandyan Kingdom.

While the English refused to concede to Dutch claims of the trade monopoly, their attempt to gain entry on the east coast of Sri Lanka made the Dutch rethink of the strategic importance of the eastern ports -- at least Trincomalee and, if permitted, Batticaloa.

Fortunately for the Dutch, according to K. M. De Silva, a rebellion against Rajasinghe in 1665 compelled him to seek Dutch assistance. Dutch troops in Kandy and naval support in the East coast enabled the Dutch to consolidate control over the entire coastline. From 1675 however, Rajasinghe recommenced attacks on the Dutch, demonstrating shrewdness in his choice of targets and eliminating much of the Dutch conquered areas. By 1677, however, the Dutch were following a policy of inactivity,  but continued to be vigilant. And Rajasinghe in his last years of reign, sought a more reconciliatory relationship with them.

Knox’s legacy

Regarded as an exception among Kandyan rulers for his commitment to a policy of resistance toward the Westerners, Rajasinghe however appeared to have changed his attitude even towards those captured. The allowance as a result gave Knox to enjoy a degree of freedom unusual for a prisoner,  and enabled him to observe Sri Lankan society with remarkable detail and sympathy.

During the nineteen years as what was termed as a “free Prisoner,” Knox cultivated crops and knitted caps spending his life as a petty trader while exploring possible escape routes.

Following the publication of his book in London, some questioned  whether Knox had truly disliked living in Sri Lanka among strangers. If his overriding ambition had been escape, how had he produced such nuanced and empathetic observations of the people, their customs and   daily life?  His illustrations even included a portrait of Rajasinghe II whom he described had a “swelling belly, somewhat bald…..and a large comely beard with great whiskers, in conclusion a very comely man.”

Knox’s years in captivity evidently had not diminished his appetite for travel. Following his escape, he had undertaken at least five more voyages to the East. He died in 1720 and lived long enough to bask in the well deserved popularity of his book. His prediction “it being ye the only thing that will keep my name in memory in ye world,” proved correct as, nearly three and a half centuries later, Robert Knox remains remembered not simply as a prisoner who escaped from the Kandyan Kingdom but as one of the most perceptive chroniclers of seventeenth century Sri Lanka whose observations continue to illuminate the island’s history, the society and parlance.

    President A. K. Questions over the first post-mortem examination soon intensified. Karachi Police Surgeon Dr Summaiyya Syed publicly raised concerns about inconsistencies in the medical findings and photographs.

The family subsequently approached a court seeking exhumation, a fresh post-mortem and further forensic examination.

A Karachi court authorised the exhumation and ordered the constitution of a medico-legal board. The process itself became controversial when the board was reconstituted shortly before the scheduled procedure, prompting objections from the family.

The second examination produced a markedly different picture. Investigators collected samples for chemical analysis, gunshot-residue examination and identification. The later forensic work included DNA analysis and laboratory examination of material evidence.

By the end of August, the final post-mortem report had ruled out suicide as the manner of death and confirmed death from a gunshot wound. The report also incorporated chemical and forensic findings.

A judicial commission subsequently began examining the circumstances surrounding Ali’s death, including alleged lapses in the original investigation and discrepancies between medical reports.

The significance of the case lies not merely in what happened to one young man. It demonstrates how an unreliable medico-legal assessment can alter the direction of an entire criminal investigation before the evidence has been properly tested.

The post-mortem is not paperwork

A post-mortem report is often treated as one document among many in a criminal case. In reality, it can determine the trajectory of an investigation.

The description of wounds, location of injuries, possible bullet trajectory, cause and manner of death, presence of toxic substances and other medical observations can influence whether investigators pursue murder, suicide, accident or another explanation.

Once an initial conclusion enters the case record, subsequent investigators can become anchored to it.

That makes inaccuracies particularly damaging.

In the Mir Raza Ali case, questions were raised over the description of the firearm injury and the relationship between the entry and exit wounds. Questions were also raised about whether gunshot-residue testing had been conducted.

The subsequent examination found injuries and evidence that complicated the original interpretation.

The episode illustrates why medico-legal medicine cannot operate as a routine administrative exercise. Every observation can eventually become evidence before a court.

Forensic gaps weaken prosecutions

Pakistan’s wider criminal justice system has repeatedly confronted problems involving investigation quality, forensic evidence and the chain of custody.

In May 2026, the Lahore High Court identified poor investigation, weak prosecution and mishandling of forensic evidence among the major reasons for low conviction rates in Punjab.

In one case, the court found that investigators had failed to properly document the handling and custody of DNA samples, verify the identities of persons presented for DNA testing and maintain an adequate chain of custody.

Such failures do not necessarily mean that the underlying evidence is false. They mean that the prosecution may become unable to establish that evidence was properly collected, preserved, identified and handled.

That distinction is crucial. Modern forensic science depends not only on sophisticated laboratory equipment but also on disciplined procedures from the crime scene to the courtroom.

A DNA profile, fingerprint, firearm component, blood sample or digital device has evidentiary value only when investigators can demonstrate how it was obtained and preserved. Any unexplained gap can provide the defence with grounds to challenge its reliability.

Crime scenes can lose the truth

The problem begins well before laboratory analysis.

Crime scenes require careful preservation, documentation, photography, mapping, evidence collection and reconstruction. If the scene is disturbed before specialists arrive, potentially decisive evidence can disappear or become contaminated.

Pakistan has long faced criticism over the limited forensic capacity available outside major centres.

A 2025 Dawn report highlighted concerns that police and emergency personnel who often reach crime scenes first may lack adequate forensic training, resulting in poor preservation and documentation.

It also pointed to weaknesses in chain-of-custody procedures and the limited availability of advanced forensic laboratories.

These deficiencies become particularly consequential in homicide investigations. A misplaced object, an improperly sealed sample, an undocumented transfer, or an incomplete crime-scene record can later create uncertainty that no laboratory can eliminate.

The forensic reconstruction of a crime requires more than collecting physical objects. Investigators must establish how those objects relate to the sequence of events.

Pakistan has created a national framework

The federal government has recognised the need for stronger forensic infrastructure.

Parliament enacted the National Forensics Agency Act, 2024, establishing a National Forensics Agency with a mandate covering forensic examination and expert opinions for courts and investigating authorities across Pakistan.

The agency's stated areas of work include DNA and serology, fingerprints, digital forensics, toxicology, firearms and tool marks, questioned documents, crime-scene investigation, pathology and explosives analysis.

The creation of a national institution represents an acknowledgement of the importance of scientific evidence. But legislation alone does not determine the reliability of investigations.

The credibility of forensic evidence ultimately depends on professional standards, qualified personnel, laboratory procedures, documentation and independence from investigative pressure.

The continued controversy surrounding medico-legal reports shows the distance between having forensic institutions on paper and ensuring that evidence is consistently handled to the required standard.

Pressure can distort the record

The most serious concern is not simply incompetence. It is the possibility that medical or forensic evidence can be influenced, altered or disputed after the fact.

Dawn’s August 2026 editorial, prompted by the Mir Raza Ali case, noted that medico-legal reports have been disputed with disturbing regularity and warned that pressure may be applied to alter evidence. An inaccurate record of injuries can fundamentally affect a victim’s case.

For families of deceased victims, the consequences are immediate. A flawed report can transform a suspected homicide into a suicide, weaken a murder prosecution or create years of legal uncertainty.

For an accused person, unreliable evidence can also have grave consequences. Forensic weakness therefore does not favour justice in either direction. It can shield a guilty person while simultaneously exposing an innocent person to suspicion or prosecution.

The credibility gap

The Mir Raza Ali investigation now includes a judicial commission examining the conduct of officials and the circumstances surrounding the death. The commission has heard testimony from medical and police personnel and received post-mortem, forensic, digital and witness material.

The fact that a disputed first examination had to be followed by exhumation, fresh forensic testing and judicial scrutiny illustrates the institutional cost of weak initial investigations.

Every subsequent examination consumes time, public resources and judicial attention. More importantly, evidence deteriorates. Biological material changes, crime scenes disappear, memories become less precise and digital information can become harder to recover.

Justice therefore depends not only on having forensic science but on applying it correctly at the earliest stage.

Pakistan’s forensic problem is consequently larger than the shortcomings of individual reports.

It concerns the credibility of the entire evidentiary chain — from the first officer entering a crime scene to the medical examiner conducting an autopsy, the laboratory analyst examining evidence and the prosecutor presenting it before a court.

When that chain breaks, the consequences extend beyond a single investigation. The reliability of the justice system itself comes under question.

The Mir Raza Ali case has placed that question in unusually sharp focus.

The final forensic determination may clarify the circumstances of one death, but the controversies surrounding the investigation have exposed a wider problem: where forensic evidence is weak, disputed or improperly handled, the path to justice becomes uncertain long before a case reaches the courtroom.
